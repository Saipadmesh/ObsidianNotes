A story holds the attention of a consumer with setups and payoffs. Every good movie has good setup and equally rewarding payoffs. heck, even every good scene in a movie has its own setup and payoff. The horror movie Undertone is so interesting to me because the payoff in that movie, or the gimmick that is supposed to haunt us, is not that interesting, but the setup is just so impressive, that I was in awe throughout most of the movie.

The movie gripped my attention right from the first minute, with its setup of a horror podcaster named Evy who lives with her mother who is in a coma. Evy works with Justin to report supernatural occurrences in a podcase called "The Undertone". One night, they investigate an anonymous email that Justin received, which contains ten voice recordings, and they play them one by one. I am not sure how they did it, but the tension buildup using the voice recordings really worked for me. The usage of children's rhymes as a plot point is a masterful move because it grounds the events of the story to the real world, and also provides this dissonance for the viewer as a way to raise tension. The breaks from the podcast recording are only present to give the viewer more context about Evy's life and serve as a breather from the main plot. Every time there was a break I felt really relieved, because the intensity of the main plot had my heart pumping hard all the time. 

The technical ways in which the movie builds tension and urgency are also interesting to note. Evy starts working on the podcast only at 2.30am in the night, which is a good excuse to have a dimly lit house. The camera mostly is fixed, except at times where there is a painfully slow pan which is used so effectively to convey suspense. Most times there's nothing important on the other side post pan. The audio reversals which play heavily into the plot is also a simple but effective technique. There is one shot where the camera focuses on a mirror which shows Evy with her laptop turned on. The laptop has the picture of a screaming woman on it. The camera fixed on a flipped image already feels so weird to look at, and then the camera starts panning to the right, but only to show the real view of Evy with her laptop. There is interestingly very little jump scares in the movie, and it also doesn't immediately jump into psychological horror to instill dread in the audience. It sticks to supernatural horror, but leaves a lot of negative space visually and plot wise, for the viewer to fill in the gaps. I saw one review that read that this movie is more scary for people who watch a lot of horror films, because a horror veteran's mind imagines much more vile scenarios to fill the gap than an average person.

The last ten minutes of the movie, though being weak plot-wise and horror wise, is a well executed scene. The camera follows Evy over the shoulder as she slowly walks up the stairs, and it's a set of stairs we've already seen before, but this time, the lights are flickering and there's a lot of children's drawings on the wall, and when Evy reaches her mom's room, we only see a silhouette in the mirror far away. The dialogue and the mystery around the plot was scarier than the actual ending, but as a scene, it is serviceable and it works. 

Overall this film had more experience than plot, but the experience was an amazing one. In a lot of ways it is similar to Oh Shit, and that gives it a sentimental attachment for me.
