In a world where acceptance is the primary driving force, ambition and desire are scorned. That is what the treatment of Indra by the Indian culture teaches us. In the puranas, Lord Indra is referenced in several stories as the god of lightning and thunder, and the king of the Devas. Similar to Zeus, he is often represented as this valorous soldier-king who also has a giant sense of ego, and as someone who would resort to deceit if things didn't go his way, like in the story where he impersonated as the sage Gautama to have coitus with his wife Ahalya. But if we go back to the period of the Vedas and look at the Rig Veda, Indra is the primary deity who is praised. One fourth of all the hymns in the Rig Veda are dedicated to him. Indra is known to help win battles and grant wealth and fame to those who worship him. He is notably known for defeating a dragon-snake named Vritra. 

All of Indra's valorous tales are overshadowed in post Vedic culture by other devatas like Vishnu, Shiva and Devi. In the Rig Veda, Vishnu is seen as a minor deity, and the name Shiva does not even appear once (there was a Rudra instead, who was the god of storms and medicines). During the Puranic and Upanishad eras, Vishnu rose to be a prominent deity and has stayed there ever since. Rudra on the other hand, amalgamated with a non Vedic god called Pasupathi and became the god who we call Shiva. Hinduism is well known for this kind of cultural assimilation of popular religious figures. A tribal god called Jagannath (who is represented as crude wooden stump) is now worshipped as a form of Vishnu/Krishna, and so is Buddha. Even Krishna himself is an amalgamation of a non Vedic clan leader called Vasudeva, and the god Narayana.
