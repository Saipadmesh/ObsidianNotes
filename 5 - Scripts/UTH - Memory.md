Abstractions are a good thing. It's not the duty of every person who wants to sketch to understand the inner workings of the pen they use. Problem: What is memory?
Memory is the ability of a thing by which data is encoded, stored and retrieved... by the brain or the computer. 

In the computer, we've all heard of this since high school. There's primary memory and secondary memory. And primary memory is divided into RAM and ROM. RAM is where every variable you type, every line of code you write, and the kernel of the OS your application runs is all loaded to during runtime.

The human brain is much more simpler on the surface. You have the long term memory, and the short term memory. If you remember hearing at least some of the phrases somewhere in your high school or college, then congrats! Your long term memory is working. This is the collection of everything you've ever consumed through your senses your entire life. The short term memory on the other hand, stores around 15-30 seconds worth of content, like the last sentence you just read. There's also the working memory, which is similar to short term memory, but is used for storing details that help you with learning and understanding.

Problem: How does it work?
I can't reasonably explain all sorts of ways humans have invented to store and process data which is not their physical brain, so I'll just stick to the most relevant one in tech, RAM. Without getting too much into the hardware side of things, RAM is just an array of individual bytes, each having their own address, like a large array. Some byte chunks are used to store your variables, others are used to store the order in which your functions should execute. When you run a program, the compiler talks to the hardware to allocate and assign some bytes, and gets you the address, which you can use to access it.

I personally think every person who wants to learn anything at all must understand how the long term memory of the brain works. When you read something new for the first time, the working memory tries to reason and understand what it's working with, and when you interact with the material more, your brain physically forms new connections with neurons. The more consistently you bang your head (figuratively) on the thing you're learning, the more connections your brain forms and the faster it grasps the concept.

Problem: Is the storage approach fool proof?
Since a computer is a tool made for humans by humans, the approach of how data is stored and retrieved from RAM works with a very less failure rate. Older programming languages gave a lot of control of what gets allocated to memory and what gets cleaned up to the programmer. But with great power comes great responsibility, and the programmer who did not know how to handle memory management used to face all sorts of memory leaks and segmentation faults. Even though modern programming languages abstract memory management to a point where an average developer doesn't necessarily have to know about it at all, in large scale systems, memory leaks can still become an issue, so there's no harm in learning about memory management.

The key point to understand about the human brain is that it is not a machine. It is not built with simple, specific memory chunks that you can use to store information and then clean up. It is built through years and years of evolution and conditioning to really optimize for survival. And survival in this case means constant exposure to problems in the subject we're trying to learn, so that the brain can strip down the subject matter to its most essential elements, form relevant connections and utilize past experience in ways you wouldn't have thought of before. The condition is that "constant" exposure doesn't mean continuous exposure without any rest, because that overloads your working memory and prevents you from moving concepts to the long term memory. Another surefire way to overload your working memory is to take up a task which is too big to handle with your current level of knowledge, and try to force your way through without any handholding. This forms the basis of the "Expertise reversal effect", and the solution is to consume some theory and immediately jump into a small problem, or a problem with some guidance, before you take on bigger problems directly.
